Closing the loop: a session utilization review process

A case study in mapping a recurring operational gap and designing the review step that closes it, built from real attendance data at a private skating facility, with names and facility identity removed.

The problem

Ice schedules at facilities like this are typically set roughly a year in advance: which days and time slots go to which activity, then which session types get assigned within those slots. Within a season, sessions are sometimes combined on an ad hoc basis when eligibility allows, but this depends entirely on someone happening to notice the opportunity. An on-site attendance monitor checks who shows up against who registered, for every session, in real time, but that role has no scheduling authority and no visibility into the broader pattern.

The result is a gap: attendance data is being collected every single session, but there is no routine path for that data to reach whoever owns the scheduling decision. A session can run chronically empty, or chronically over capacity, for months without it surfacing unless someone independently notices and raises it.

Current state

Mapping the process as it actually runs, rather than as it's assumed to run, is usually where the real finding lives.

Evidence the gap has a real cost

An independent analysis of one month of session data found 42 recurring time slots, each appearing 3 or more times in the month, averaging under 15% of assumed capacity. These are not one-off slow weeks: they are the same slot, week after week, consistently underfilled, with no evidence in the data that anything happens as a result.

Proposed future state

The fix doesn't require new data collection, the attendance data already exists every session. It requires a defined review step that reads that data on a regular cadence and routes findings to two places: back into the current season for in-season adjustments, and forward into the next annual scheduling cycle as a documented input rather than institutional memory.

Proposed decision rule

  • Review cadence: monthly during the active season.
  • Low-utilization trigger: a time slot averaging below 15% of assumed capacity across 3 or more consecutive occurrences is flagged and routed to whoever holds scheduling authority for a decision on whether to combine, adjust, or reassign it.
  • High-utilization trigger: a time slot averaging above 90% of assumed capacity across 2 or more consecutive occurrences is flagged as a candidate for added capacity, routed the same way.
  • Every flagged pattern is logged, whether or not it results in an in-season change, so it becomes a documented input to the next annual allocation cycle instead of relying on someone remembering.

A note on decision authority

This project maps where the gap is and what data would close it. It does not assume authority over how any specific organization is governed, scheduling decisions might sit with staff, a volunteer board, or both, and that structure is intentionally left generic here. The review step's job is to make sure a well-documented pattern reliably reaches whoever holds that authority, on a predictable schedule, rather than depending on someone happening to notice. Whether the next step after a flag is an immediate change or an item on a board agenda is a separate, organization-specific question, and it doesn't change the underlying case: without this step, the data isn't reliably reaching anyone at all.

Why this needs a defined owner

This review step doesn't fit neatly into either existing function. It isn't the attendance monitor's job, that role is operational and per-session by design. It isn't naturally anyone's job in the annual scheduling process either, since that process runs on a roughly year-long cycle with no built-in mechanism to incorporate in-season data. A defined periodic review, owned by a single accountable role, is what turns data that already exists into decisions that already have a clear owner.
